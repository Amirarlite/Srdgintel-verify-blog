## The promise versus the numbers

When the current APC administration took office in 2023, it inherited an economy already under strain and moved quickly on two major reforms: removing the longstanding fuel subsidy and floating the naira. Those changes were framed as the difficult but necessary path to long-term stability. Three years on, the macroeconomic indicators the government points to — a strengthening currency, moderating inflation, projected GDP growth of 4.2% — have not translated into better conditions for most households, according to the World Bank's own assessment.

## What the poverty data shows

The World Bank's April 2026 Nigeria Development Update puts the national poverty rate at 63% in 2025, up from 61% in 2024 and 56% in 2023. In raw numbers, that's an increase from roughly 104 million Nigerians below the poverty line in 2023 to an estimated 140 million in 2025. The Bank's own projection has poverty peaking near 62–63% in 2026 before a modest decline to 61% in 2027 and 59% by 2028.

### Where the pressure is concentrated

Agriculture, which employs more than half of Nigeria's poor, has grown far more slowly than the services and industrial sectors now driving headline GDP figures.

## Inflation: down on paper, still painful at the till

Headline inflation has genuinely eased — from a peak near 34.8% in December 2024 to 15.15% by December 2025. But the relief has been uneven: food inflation fell as low as single digits in early 2026 before climbing back to 17.52% by June, and the cost of a healthy diet reached ₦1,589 per adult per day in April 2026, up nearly 5% year-on-year.

## The government's response — and its pushback

The administration's policy response has included the minimum wage increase, the student loan and consumer credit scheme, CNG conversion subsidies, and reliance on the Dangote refinery. Officials have also publicly disputed the World Bank's poverty figures, calling them unreliable. Independent of that dispute, the trend across multiple releases of the same series — and separately confirmed by NBS inflation data — has been consistent: macro stabilization first, household relief lagging behind it.

## What to watch

The World Bank's forecast has poverty reduction starting only in 2026 or later, contingent on sustained growth, continued disinflation, and expanded social protection holding up against risks like oil-price shocks and climate-driven food disruption.
